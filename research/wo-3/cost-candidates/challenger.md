# Challenger O-ring launch-constraint decision — WO-3 cost-boundary evidence record

**Candidate ID:** `challenger-o-ring-launch-constraint`
**Strict selection result:** **DOES NOT QUALIFY.** The candidate is retained as a **partial** case because it fails Gate 3 of the preregistered five-gate rule.
**Control assessed:** A temperature-based launch no-go recommendation for the Solid Rocket Motor (SRM) field-joint O-rings. Thiokol engineers' stated condition was that launch should be consistent with the lowest previous launch temperature, 53 °F; they initially recommended against launching on the scheduled morning. This record assesses that discrete January 27–28, 1986 decision, not the entire Challenger design-history or post-accident redesign program. [1]

## Decision and declared boundary

**DERIVED — decision.** Challenger is **not** a qualifying WO-3 cost case. Gates 1, 2, 4 (with a limited risk/counterfactual meaning), and 5 pass; Gate 3 fails. Because the rule is conjunctive, the failure is dispositive. The evidence establishes a real launch-control reversal, an authoritative connection to risk and the January 28 approval decision, and reproducible downstream public-budget categories. It does not establish an ex-ante dollar saving, cost of delay, quantified schedule value, or explicit cost/schedule rationale adopted for the reversal.

The declared system is the STS-51-L SRM field-joint launch-readiness decision. The decision boundary is the NASA–Morton Thiokol process of January 27–28, 1986. The accounting boundary records selected NASA, contractor, and federal-appropriation categories only. The documented fiscal-category horizon runs from the January 27 teleconference through FY1987. It is not an estimate of total federal cost, total social cost, or the value of the seven deaths, injuries, disrupted missions, or all third-party effects.

All dollar amounts retain the reporting basis and year used by their source. No currency conversion or inflation adjustment is made. Every cost category below is **non-additive** for this record: it may be reproduced only within its stated source and boundary, and it may not be allocated to the launch decision, assigned an attribution ratio, or combined with another category absent a line-item reconciliation.

## Decision record, causal scope, and cost-link gap

**OBSERVED — available control and reversal.** The Commission's account of the January 27 teleconference says that Thiokol engineering recommended a launch condition consistent with the lowest prior launch temperature, 53 °F, and initially recommended against launching the following morning as scheduled. After the company caucus, Thiokol returned with a recommendation to proceed. The Commission also found that the relevant decision-makers did not know the contractor's initial written recommendation or the continuing engineering opposition. [1]

**OBSERVED — accident mechanism.** The Commission identifies destruction of the seals in the right SRM aft field joint as the failure mechanism. Its technical findings state that a combustion-gas leak through that joint initiated at or shortly after ignition and ultimately led to structural breakup and loss of the vehicle. The Commission excluded the other Shuttle elements, payload, launch-site operations, and sabotage as contributors to that field-joint leak. [2]

**DERIVED — limited causal conclusion.** The record supports the proposition that declining the engineers' control left the January 28 decision exposed to the identified field-joint risk, and that complete material information would highly likely have changed that decision. It does not establish the stronger all-world counterfactual that a temperature no-go was the sole cause of the loss or would certainly have prevented it. The House investigation's finding that schedule and cost were prioritized over safety in the broader pre-accident joint-design history remains relevant context, but it is retrospective program history rather than a contemporaneous rationale for this reversal. [4]

**OBSERVED — methodological context, not causal authority.** A later peer-reviewed reanalysis reports 23 earlier launches at 53–81 °F and a planned Challenger launch at 31 °F. It treats the inference as a large extrapolation in forecasting O-ring damage. This supports the need for cautious inference; it is not the causal authority for Gate 4. [9]

**OBSERVED — broader redesign history.** An August 1985 Thiokol task-force record, reproduced in the Commission report, described longer-term measures that would take years and recommended simpler short-term measures to reduce flight risk. The House investigation found that the joint problem had been recognized early enough to redesign and manufacture new joints before the accident and criticized the failure to adopt an early redesign recommendation. [4] [8] These facts make the control environment technically concrete, but they do not supply a decision-specific price or schedule value for the January 27 reversal.

**OBSERVED — corrected hearing evidence.** Post-accident Commission hearing testimony did include an illustrative amount. A witness said the issue had not been “scoped moneywise,” then used “maybe $5, $10 million” for extra testing and improvement. That testimony is post-accident, hypothetical and unscoped; it concerns testing and improvement rather than the January 27 reversal, and it was not adopted by the responsible decision-maker. It therefore does not satisfy Gate 3. [7]

**OBSERVED — later trade-off, excluded from Gate 3.** A NASA history describes a post-accident decision not to build a vertical full-scale SRM test stand because it would have cost $20 million and delayed the next launch by at least one year. It is an authentic later cost/schedule trade-off, but it cannot be repurposed as the pre-accident cost of the 53 °F launch constraint or of a pre-accident redesign. [10]

**UNRESOLVED — missing decision-specific value.** The 1985 CBO pricing report is program-wide analysis, not a January 1986 decision document. Its summary gives a full operational cost of $84 million per flight, while Table 7 gives an $85 million NASA-base average operational cost per flight, both in 1982 dollars. This one-million-dollar internal presentation difference is retained rather than resolved. Neither figure is the cost of delaying STS-51-L and neither supports an allocation, attribution ratio, or claimed saving for the January 27 decision. [3]

## Bounded selection null, falsifier, and next evidence

**DERIVED — bounded cost null.** Within this candidate, the declared January 27–FY1987 horizon, and the preregistered five-gate rule, the selection result is a null: no qualifying cost case is established. This bounded null does not show that cost was never a legitimate input, that the disaster had no downstream cost, or that some unreviewed decision record cannot satisfy Gate 3.

**PLANNED — falsifier of the present Gate 3 failure.** The candidate would qualify only if a contemporaneous NASA, Thiokol, or mission-management record tied the January 27–28 reversal to at least one of the following: a stated cost of delay or scrub; a stated saving from retaining the launch date; a quantified schedule effect whose value is specified; or an explicit cost/schedule rationale adopted by the person or body that reversed the no-go recommendation. A general flight-rate target, a retrospective finding about pressure, a post-accident estimate, or a program-wide operational-cost figure would not suffice.

**PLANNED — targeted retrieval.** The next search should seek the original teleconference artifacts, caucus notes, and mission-management decision memoranda; the primary 1987 NASA–Thiokol settlement; and auditable line-item records capable of reconciling CBO appropriations, GAO-counted changes, and contractor work. Until then, the decision-specific cost field and all cross-category overlap remain **UNRESOLVED**.

## Ontology and scope limit

**DERIVED — narrowed ontology conclusion.** These three examples do not certify a whole-life terminal instance under the declared crossing metric. They do **not** establish that no terminal physical system exists. The literal exact-zero condition is narrower: every possible channel would need an exactly zero upper bound for all time, under a complete ontology, boundary, horizon, and physical model. Empirical observation normally involves an information or energy coupling somewhere, which limits empirical certification of universal zero; it does not logically prove that an unobserved terminal system is impossible. [21]

**UNRESOLVED — universal terminality claim.** “No crossing detected,” a finite-horizon detection bound, and a universal exact-zero upper bound are distinct results. Only the third satisfies the work order's literal falsifier. No example here supplies it. The resulting conclusion is therefore limited to the named examples and declared frames, while the cost-case conclusion remains the bounded Gate-3 null above.
