# Validation record — Deepwater Horizon cancelled cement-evaluation log

**Item:** `deepwater-horizon-cancelled-cement-evaluation-log`
**Independent validation date:** 19 September 2026
**Verdict:** **Needs correction; not publication-ready.**

## Decision

The evidence record is right to reject the folklore claim that avoiding a **US$128,000** cement-evaluation-log fee *but-for caused* the Deepwater Horizon losses, and it is right not to add the downstream ledger. It is **not** adequate as written because it silently gives a 2011 Joint Investigation Team (JIT) finding priority over a contrary 2014 federal-court finding, mis-cites two National Academies conditional statements, and overlooks the court’s cost-and-schedule evidence.

On the five preregistered gates, the candidate **does not qualify on the materials currently identified**, but the defensible strict reason is narrower: a decision-time cost document has not been produced. Gate 4 cannot be marked “not met” without disclosing that the federal court found the cancelled log a **substantial cause**. That finding is a causal connection, not proof that the log would certainly have prevented every loss; no dollar amount may be attributed to the cancelled log.

> **Retained finding:** The $128,000 decision-boundary amount must not be compared arithmetically with later spill costs, and no downstream amount may be transferred to the cancelled-log decision without an allocation or a valid causal model. The case remains non-qualifying only if the protocol’s contemporaneous-cost requirement is applied literally and is not cured by a later-produced decision record.

## Factual and citation corrections

The core control and decision facts are supported. The Commission says BP sent the three-person Schlumberger team home and thereby saved time and the $128,000 fee.[1] The JIT supports the positioned crew, the remedial function of a log, the 20 April decision, the decision-tree criteria, and the inconsistency with BP’s listed “proven cement evaluation techniques.”[2] BP’s own investigation supports the separate statement that a formal risk assessment **might** have identified a cement-evaluation log as a mitigation option; it is not proof of prevention.[5]

The record’s citation to the final National Academies report is too strong. That report says the float-collar location would have prevented the log from investigating the lower cemented sections. It says the top of cement and quality above the float collar could have been determined **if** the necessary foam-cement-evaluation software was on board.[4] The record’s phrasing that a log “could have helped reveal that cementing had not gone as planned,” and its assertion that meaningful flow oversight “probably” would have led to further diagnostic tests, are from the Academies’ **2010 interim report**, not the cited 2012 final report.[10] The final report should either be quoted at its narrower conditional scope or the interim report should be added as a separate reference.

The record’s statement that no reviewed primary source supplied a specific duration or related cost is false once its own cited federal-court record is checked. The court’s footnote reports BP testimony of approximately **10–12 hours**, approximately **$128,000** to run the log, plus approximately **$400,000** for Schlumberger’s time; it also records Schlumberger’s estimate of less than **$200,000**.[3] These mutually non-identical estimates must not be collapsed into a single “saving.” They do, however, falsify the statement that no primary source gave a tied duration or cost figure.

The downstream-ledger labels are generally sound if kept as separate accounting/legal categories. “BP direct organizational accounting charge” should be read as a **2010 financial-statement charge**, not a cash payment on 20 April. GAO’s payment figure is a dated reimbursement snapshot, not an all-time total.[6] The 2015 DOJ release announced the proposed global civil resolution; the consent decree was judicially approved on 4 April 2016.[8] [9] This is a date/status correction, not a new amount.

The original operating comparison has a reasonably stated immediate boundary—run a log versus time and the reported fee—but the proposed redraw is **not** a causal damage estimate. Its horizon mixes an April 2010 operating decision, a 2010 accounting charge, an October 2010 reimbursement snapshot, and a 2015/2016 civil resolution. That is permissible for a non-additive illustration only if the dates and category boundaries remain explicit. It cannot establish that the later items are the cost of cancelling the log.

## Source conflicts that must be published, not resolved silently

1. **Causation:** The 2011 JIT found the annular cement did not fail and therefore the unperformed log was not a cause of the well failure.[2] The 2014 federal court found the log would have shown misplaced cement and that not running it was a substantial cause of the blowout, explosion, and spill.[3] These are directly incompatible log-specific causal findings. The court finding is later and judicial, but neither authority establishes the stronger claim that the log would certainly have prevented all harm.
2. **Log reach and inference:** The final National Academies report limits a potential log to the top of cement/annulus above the float collar and conditions even that capability on software; it excludes lower productive sections.[4] Its interim report conditionally says the log could have helped reveal a problem and that better flow oversight probably would have prompted further diagnostics.[10] The evidence record conflates those versions while citing only the final report.
3. **Cost scope:** The Commission reports a $128,000 fee.[1] The court records BP testimony of $128,000 to run plus $400,000 for Schlumberger time, alongside Schlumberger’s estimate below $200,000.[3] These sources do not identify a common cost scope. Retain $128,000 only as the Commission’s reported fee, not as a verified total avoided cost.
4. **Settlement status:** DOJ’s 5 October 2015 source describes the global civil resolution as an announced settlement with a proposed consent decree.[8] DOJ’s 4 April 2016 release records judicial approval.[9] Cite the correct event date and status.

## Asymptote and ontology checks

This is a **cost candidate, not an isolation asymptote**, so it supplies no declared system `S`, boundary `B`, observation horizon `H`, crossing vector, detection bound, or terminality classification. The asymptote test is therefore not applicable as a classification exercise. If it were improperly presented as a terminal system, positive observed crossings remain: the Schlumberger crew and decision process are human/institutional crossings, and the documented public-response, corporate-accounting, and legal obligations demonstrate external dependency/exposure over stated horizons.[1] [6] [7] [8] That is enough to reject terminality for any boundary and horizon that includes them; it is not a quantified crossing-rate result.

No empirical observation in this case can certify that every possible crossing is zero. Observation can establish positive events and finite detection bounds; it cannot transform “no further crossing detected” into universal zero. The record does not itself make that error, but its proposed accounting-boundary redraw is **not** empirical proof of the work order’s ontology and must not be presented as one. Any universal zero-crossing result would require the metric’s complete-channel inventory and zero upper bounds, or an explicitly model-conditional proof—not observation alone.

## Publication disposition

Do **not** publish the record unchanged. A corrected version may retain the non-qualification result only as: **“Not selected under the strict protocol because a contemporaneous decision-cost record has not been demonstrated; causal evidence is conflicted, with the federal court finding substantial causation and the JIT finding no causation.”** It must remove the claim that the JIT finding controls, correct the National Academies citations, acknowledge the court’s 10–12-hour/cost evidence, and retain the prohibition on cost transfer, overlapping totals, and but-for causation.
