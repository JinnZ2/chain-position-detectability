# WO-3 medium audit — energy, momentum, radiation, and field coupling

**Item:** WO-3 / crossing ontology / energy, momentum, radiation, and field coupling
**Verdict:** **INCOMPLETE.** The metric's single row, “Energy and momentum,” is not a usable complete medium inventory. It combines quantities with incompatible dimensions, treats causal coupling as though it were always a transfer, and does not require mode resolution for radiation. This is an amendment, not a defence of the work order.

## What was reviewed

The work order's falsifier requires zero crossings “in every medium.” The preregistered metric defines a crossing more broadly as a transfer **or causal coupling** that changes or constrains something across the boundary. Its energy/momentum row then asks for one cumulative quantity, with examples including heat, work, radiation, force, vibration, and field-mediated transfer, expressed in joules, watts, or newton-seconds. Those choices are not commensurable: force is the *rate of linear-momentum transfer*; torque is the *rate of angular-momentum transfer*; work is energy only along an actual displacement; and a static interaction can constrain an exterior body while transferring neither net energy nor net linear momentum during the selected horizon.

The first two rows alone falsify the claim that the existing quantity field is a complete operational specification. A system can transmit angular momentum while a scalar energy record hides it. A system can also constrain an external state through a static or quasistatic field without a nonzero energy flux over the declared interval. The metric's broad prose arguably *mentions* field mediation, but its required quantities and units do not provide a way to inventory or bound that class. Mention is not measurement coverage.

## Boundary-and-horizon test

For a fixed spatial boundary \(B\) and finite horizon \(H\), the amended physical vector should not be a scalar `Q_energy,momentum`. It needs at least: (i) energy \(E\) and power history \(P(t)\); (ii) linear impulse vector \(\mathbf J=\int_H\mathbf Fdt\), preferably surface-stress resolved; (iii) angular impulse vector \(\mathbf K=\int_H\boldsymbol\tau dt\); (iv) radiative energy resolved by species and mode; and (v) a field-interaction witness such as \(\mathbf F(t),\boldsymbol\tau(t)\), field/response configuration, and exterior state constraint. Absolute inbound and outbound components should be retained rather than netted. The work order's rate conversion applies separately to each cumulative quantity; it cannot turn a force, a torque, and joules into one rate.

A positive result is practical: the torque experiment, MEMS force experiment, and thermal-transfer experiments are examples of finite-horizon witnesses. A negative result is necessarily narrower: it is an upper interval under a stated apparatus, mechanism inventory, spatial geometry, bandwidth, and horizon. ISO 11929 defines a detection limit as the smallest true quantity detectable by the applied procedure and ties characteristic limits to specified probabilities of wrong decisions; it does not convert a nondetection into an exact zero. [7] NIST likewise requires uncertainty components, their evaluation, and the basis for any probability interpretation to accompany a measurement result. [8]

## Can an empirical test verify an exact zero without an information crossing?

**No, not for an externally confirmed claim about a bounded system.** This conclusion is narrower than “measurement must dissipate positive energy.” Sagawa and Ueda's peer-reviewed theoretical analysis models measurement as creating correlation (mutual information) between a measured system and a memory/observer; it also notes special cases in which measurement heat dissipation can be zero. [9] Thus a zero-heat measurement is not a loophole: to warrant an observation outside \(S\), a record or correlation must become available outside \(S\). Under this metric's own definition, that observation/information relation is a crossing or causal coupling across \(B\).

An internal detector avoids an external crossing only by withholding its result. When its record is read out, the information crossing occurs after the selected \(H\); it therefore may leave the limited statement “no detected transfer during the earlier interval” logically intact, but it cannot establish **whole-life terminality**. Remote observation is not an exception: photon, gravitational, electromagnetic, or other coupling used to learn the result is the very crossing at issue. A detector that is truly decoupled supplies no empirical evidence.

A proof that specified laws and ideal initial/boundary conditions entail zero for enumerated modes can be valuable, but it is **model-level/deductive**, not empirical verification of a real candidate. It also cannot establish “every possible medium” unless the model supplies a closed, justified ontology. The LIGO null result illustrates the proper empirical form: no evidence in scope, followed by a confidence-qualified nonzero upper limit, rather than a verified zero. [5]

## Decision

Replace the present row with separate **energy**, **linear momentum**, **angular momentum**, **radiation-by-species-and-mode**, and **field-mediated interaction/constraint** ledgers. Add entropy/exergy only as derived contextual accounting. A candidate may be classified nonterminal when any ledger yields a positive lower witness. It must remain **unresolved**, not terminal, when its field-mechanism inventory is open, its radiation modes/species are not stated, or any upper interval remains positive or unknown. The literal zero-crossing falsifier is not empirically executable without creating at least an information/measurement crossing; it may only be posed as a conditional mathematical construction.
