# WO-1 Evidence Record — TOP10-02

**Standard:** OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications 2026, version 1.0
**Audited unit:** **ASI03: Identity and Privilege Abuse** through **ASI04: Agentic Supply Chain Vulnerabilities**
**Audit conclusion:** **NEGATIVE**
**Inside-agent requirement found:** **No**
**Prescriptive clauses reviewed:** **18 numbered Prevention and Mitigation Guidelines** (ASI03: 9; ASI04: 9).

## Finding

The audited unit contains controls for agent identities, delegated permissions, signed intent, inter-agent-message authentication, component provenance, runtime lineage monitoring, and related supply-chain controls. **None of its prescriptive clauses requires that the executing agent receive or be able to query evidence of its predecessor, successor, chain depth, chain membership, or an equivalent execution-chain position.** The closest controls require a monitoring function to observe delegation chains or lineage. The text does not expose either observation to the executing agent. Under the stated criterion, that is **negative**, not positive.

The result is not based on an inference that no implementation could expose this data. It is limited to what this standard requires. A conforming implementation can place the cited monitoring, authorization, audit, and telemetry functions outside the agent execution boundary while giving the agent no chain-position information.

## Source and audit boundary

I read the authoritative, version-pinned **57-page PDF**, rather than a search-result excerpt. The downloaded file has SHA-256 `a2db94cd00b08e0b3a5e5b619afe024bdbcd74503111085705e4f3dd886fcb5c`. The audited entry spans printed Pages 15–17 for ASI03 and printed Pages 18–20 for ASI04. PDF pages in the locations below are one greater than the document’s printed-page number. The next PDF page begins ASI05, which confirms the ASI04 boundary.[1]

For completeness, the document labels these items “Prevention and Mitigation Guidelines,” rather than using a formal RFC-style `MUST`/`SHALL` taxonomy. This record therefore treats every numbered, action-directing guideline as a **prescriptive clause** and reviews all 18 inclusively. This errs toward review rather than excluding softer directives such as “Evaluate.”

> **Criterion applied.** A positive result requires a normative requirement that places evidence of execution-chain position **inside the executing agent**: for example, the agent must receive or query its predecessor, successor, depth, membership, or equivalent location in a larger chain. Gateway-side lineage, dashboards, logs, middleware traces, and post-hoc monitoring remain negative unless the requirement expressly makes them available inside that boundary.

## Decisive evidence and locus analysis

The strongest evidence against a positive classification is the pair of clauses that expressly reach chain-adjacent information but allocate it to observation rather than in-agent knowledge. ASI03 ¶8 requires: “**Monitor when an agent gains new permissions indirectly through delegation chains**.” ASI04 ¶6 requires: “**monitor behavior, privilege use, lineage, and inter-module telemetry for anomalies**.”[1] These statements establish that the standard recognizes delegation chains and lineage as objects of monitoring. They do not require the monitored agent itself to receive or retrieve its lineage, its relation to the predecessor or successor, its depth, or its membership.

The unit’s other identity and supply-chain controls similarly operate at identifiable non-topological loci: a central policy engine; identity and access management; token binding and verification; human approval; supply-chain registries and inventories; installation gatekeeping; build and release processes; runtime monitoring; and deployment-wide revocation. Even ASI04’s message-authentication clause concerns authenticity and attestation of messages, not the receiver’s execution-chain location.

The descriptions and examples do not alter that conclusion. ASI03 describes attacks that manipulate “delegation chains,” and its examples describe cross-agent trust and inherited permission. ASI04 says dependencies can introduce behavior into an “agent’s execution chain,” describes runtime coordination across agents, and gives multi-agent and registry examples.[1] Those passages explain risk. They are not prescriptive clauses, and they do not impose in-boundary visibility of chain position.

## Classification rationale

**Result: negative.** No reviewed prescriptive clause satisfies the necessary condition for a positive result. The two clauses that mention delegation chains or lineage are especially decisive because they require monitoring, not agent-side access. They therefore fall squarely within the criterion’s exclusion for gateway-side lineage, middleware traces, audit/monitoring functions, and external observability.

This is not classified as ambiguous. The clauses leave implementation locus partly open in ordinary architectural terms, but none has unclear language about whether chain-position information must be exposed inside the executing agent: none requires such exposure at all. If a future revision said that each agent must query its predecessor, delegation depth, or workflow lineage before acting, that revision would require a fresh assessment and could meet the positive test.

## Limitations

This record evaluates only the normative/prescriptive content of the two designated entries in version 1.0. It does not test product behavior, infer an implementation from an OWASP example, or treat an external platform’s optional capabilities as a standard requirement. The phrase “lineage” in ASI04 ¶6 is undefined in the audited entry; the negative conclusion does not depend on defining it, because the text still requires only monitoring and does not require delivery of lineage to the executing agent. “Agent” can also refer broadly to a system in ordinary language; this audit applies the work-order’s narrower execution-boundary criterion.
