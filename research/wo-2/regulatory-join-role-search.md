# WO-2 step 4 — Regulatory existence search for an assigned cross-signal join role

**Question.** Does any regulator explicitly assign cross-signal aggregation to a named role or function with authority?

**Result: positive.** This bounded search found two clear qualifying examples in primary or official regulatory guidance: the FAA-required **designated safety-management personnel** working for the accountable executive, and the EU **Qualified Person Responsible for Pharmacovigilance (QPPV)**. In both, the source links a named role or function to organization-wide/system-wide integration of heterogeneous safety information and also gives it an escalation, reporting, or corrective/regulatory-action route. The FAA example is the clearest U.S. regulatory match.

> **Strict test used.** A result counted as positive only where an official source (1) names an individual role or defined function, (2) assigns it responsibility for integration of safety signals across organizational holders or systems—not merely custody of a record, audit, or single risk assessment—and (3) supplies a route of authority to **escalate, report, stop, or correct**. Requirements imposed only on “the employer,” “owner or operator,” or an investigation team without a named cross-signal owner did not qualify.

This is an **existence search**, not a prevalence estimate or a claim that such roles are universally mandated. It does not change the WO-2 codebook’s case-specific `join_assigned` rule: the relevant question for an accident record remains whether a role with responsibility **and authority to aggregate the relevant pre-event fragments** was actually assigned before that event.

## Search boundaries and method

The review opened full official sources across aviation, public transit, medicines/pharmacovigilance, maritime operations, chemical process safety, and workplace process safety. It favored current government-published CFR editions and the regulator’s own guidance. I searched for the conjunction of: named accountable/designated role; integration, monitoring, or overview across systems or sources; and a reporting, corrective, escalation, or operational-authority consequence. A source that only required collection, records, an audit, a PHA, or an incident investigation was coded **non-qualifying**, not proof of a regulatory absence outside the texts reviewed.

### 1. FAA aviation SMS: why it passes

The FAA rule gives the accountable executive final authority over the certificate holder’s authorized operations, control of the required financial and human resources, and ultimate responsibility for safety performance. It then requires that executive to designate sufficient management personnel who, **on the executive’s behalf**, are responsible for coordinating implementation, maintenance, and **integration** of the SMS throughout the organization; facilitating hazard identification and safety-risk analysis; monitoring control effectiveness; and regularly reporting SMS performance and necessary improvements to the accountable executive.[1]

The “integration” term is not isolated from information. Part 5 defines safety assurance as systematic performance/effectiveness processes operating “through the collection, analysis, and assessment of information.” It requires the organization to acquire data through multiple channels—operational-process and environmental monitoring, audits, evaluations, incident/accident investigations, non-compliance investigations, and a confidential employee-reporting system—and to analyze that acquired data **and any other relevant data**.[1] The accountable executive must regularly review safety performance and “direct actions necessary” to address substandard performance; assessments that find ineffective controls or new hazards feed back into risk management, and the certificate holder must correct assessed deficiencies.[1]

Read together, the rule does more than establish records or generic risk review. It assigns an organization-wide SMS-integration function, connects it to multiple independent signal holders/systems, and supplies a specified reporting/escalation route to a role with final operational authority and a duty to direct corrective action. The rule does **not** require the same natural person to perform each data analysis; its affirmative model is a named management **function** integrated under the accountable executive.

### 2. EMA QPPV: why it passes

EMA’s official GVP Module I states that the QPPV is responsible for establishing and maintaining the marketing-authorisation holder’s pharmacovigilance system, must have sufficient authority to influence pharmacovigilance activities and the quality system, and must be positioned to ensure and verify the currency and accuracy of the pharmacovigilance-system master file.[2] Its specified responsibilities include an overview of product safety profiles and emerging safety concerns; authority over risk-management-plan content; ensuring complete and correct safety data are submitted to national authorities and the Agency; providing information relevant to benefit-risk evaluation; and providing input into regulatory action responding to emerging safety concerns, including **urgent safety restrictions**.[2]

The source makes the cross-system character explicit: the QPPV has oversight over the pharmacovigilance system in all relevant respects, including standard operating procedures, contractual arrangements, database operations, compliance data on expedited and periodic reporting, audit reports, and training. Tasks may be delegated only under QPPV supervision, with the QPPV retaining system oversight and an overview of safety profiles for all products.[2] EMA’s companion signal-management module further requires a marketing-authorisation holder that becomes aware of an emerging safety issue **from any source** to notify the Agency and the competent authorities as soon as possible and no later than three working days after determining that it meets the definition.[3]

The QPPV provision therefore supplies both elements sought: a named integrator of disparate pharmacovigilance information and a practical official escalation/action channel. The notification duty in Module IX is phrased to the marketing-authorisation holder rather than personally to the QPPV; the positive conclusion rests on Module I’s explicit assignment of system oversight, safety-profile overview, submission responsibilities, authority to influence activities, and role in urgent regulatory action.

## Corroborating but not needed for the positive conclusion

### U.S. public transit: a near/direct regulatory analogue

FTA’s Public Transportation Agency Safety Plan regulation contains a strong structural analogue. An **Accountable Executive** is a single identifiable person with ultimate responsibility for the safety plan and control or direction over the human and capital resources needed to develop and maintain it. The transit agency must give that executive accountability for effective agency-wide SMS implementation and for action needed to address substandard SMS performance. It must also designate a **Chief Safety Officer or SMS Executive** with authority and responsibility for day-to-day SMS implementation and operation, reporting directly to the Accountable Executive.[4]

The regulated SMS must include safety risk management and safety assurance. The latter includes investigations of safety events, monitoring internal reporting information, monitoring procedure sufficiency and risk mitigations, and continuous-improvement identification of SMS deficiencies; safety-risk identification expressly includes oversight-authority data and information plus safety-assurance concerns.[4] For large providers, the joint Safety Committee has access to agency information, resources, tools, and worker safety reports, identifies risk mitigations and deficiencies, and transmits recommendations into the Accountable Executive’s mandatory consideration/implementation structure.[4]

This was retained as **corroborating** rather than necessary evidence because the regulation does not use the single verb “integrate” for the Chief Safety Officer/SMS Executive in the way FAA Part 5 does. On the whole it is a plausible additional positive: the named SMS executive has operational authority over the system that joins these inputs, while the named Accountable Executive has resource and corrective-action accountability. The FAA and EMA examples are sufficiently explicit without resolving this interpretive edge.

These non-qualifications are not claims that aviation, maritime, chemical, or workplace organisations lack people who perform such work. They record only that the examined provisions did not meet the deliberately strict, source-textual test.

## Implication for WO-2

The existence result **falsifies any universal claim** that safety regulation cannot explicitly locate the aggregation/join function in a named role with authority. FAA Part 5 demonstrates a regulatory design in which multi-channel safety assurance is integrated through designated management personnel and escalated to an accountable executive empowered to direct corrective action. EMA GVP demonstrates a comparable safety-information governance design in pharmacovigilance.

The result does **not** establish that the function was assigned in any particular CSB accident report, that every regulator uses the same allocation, or that a named SMS/QPPV role actually received the relevant fragments before an event. For case coding, retain the codebook’s demanding evidentiary rule: mark `join_assigned = yes` only when the incident record itself supports both a designated role/function and authority to aggregate the particular pre-event cross-holder signals.[8]

## Limitations

1. This was a bounded legal/guidance existence search, not a systematic census of all jurisdictions, all regulated sectors, or every revision of every source.
2. “Authority” is operationally heterogeneous. FAA supplies reporting to a final operational authority; EMA supplies influence, submission/contact duties, and a regulatory-action pathway. Neither should be paraphrased as an unconditional personal power to shut down every operation.
3. The EMA examples are official regulatory guidance that cites EU legal instruments; they are not U.S. statutory text. They are included because the task permits official guidance and because the role allocation is unusually explicit.
4. Search results were not used as evidence. The findings above are based on the full official texts listed below; eCFR pages that blocked automated extraction were replaced with official GovInfo editions or the issuing regulator’s PDFs.
